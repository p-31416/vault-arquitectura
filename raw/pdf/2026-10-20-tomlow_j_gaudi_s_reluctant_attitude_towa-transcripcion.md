The hanging model that Antoni Gaudi (1852-1926) developed for the Colonia Giiell church design has been the subject

of considerable research and its importance as an experiment for the design of Sagrada Familia has been noted.

Critical opinions and assessments, however, include a misunderstanding of the physical laws and geometry involved

in the hanging model process and the legend that Gaudi was obsessed with designing stone structures via hanging



models, thus rejecting other approaches. Such opinions were countered by Gaudi himself, who talked of the problems



involved in interpreting hanging models, The renown of the hanging model has also invoked scepticism on the part of

civil engineers, art historians and architects as to the theoretical profundity of Gaudi’s design methodology. This

paper offers a state-of-the-art survey of this topic, based both on specialist literature sources and on discussions



conducted with leading experts since 1976. It can be concluded that Gaudi was aware of the theoretical impact of the



hanging model, within its historic parameters, and his attitude may inspire current construction design.

3. The hanging model



3.1 The hanging model produces force lines, not

surfaces



One of the first books on Gaudi to be published outside Spain

was Antonio Gaudi by George R. Collins (1917-1993) in 1960

(Collins, 1960). Although Collins is known as an excellent

writer, his description of the hanging model process reveals

erroneous interpretations. The relevant textual passages are

supplemented by captions to illustrations of the hanging

model. On p. 24, Collins writes: ‘By suspending weights from

sheets instead of from cords, the properly warped surfaces were

obtained (plate 62)... The system has application to modern

concrete construction’. The caption to plate 62 reads: ‘Inverted

photograph of the funicular model for the Colonia Giell

chapel hanging in the workshop beside the building. This

represents the sheet-calculations for exterior surfaces’. And the

caption to plate 63 reads: ‘Inverted photograph of the interior

The main problem revealed by these comments is that the white

sheets of paper or thin cloth visible on the original photographs

have been mistaken for structural parts of the hanging model

system. In fact, these sheets were added by Gaudi in order to give

the visual suggestion of wall surfaces. At the same time, they

concealed the disturbingly visible workshop (Figures 2 and 5). In

this way, Gaudi could easily draw in the completed architecture

on top of the inverted photographs of the hanging model.



Collins suggests that the hanging model may be considered to

be a synthetic calculating device, which is a correct assumption.

Physically speaking, the model represents a static equilibrium

of forces as intended for the design, which — with the right

methods and tools — may give the required design data. From

the hanging model the following data may be derived:

We may ask ourselves what Gaudi himself would think about

this discussion — whether he used the hanging model only to

express linear forces and thus not for any direct translation of

forces within a slab-like body such as a vault or a wall part.

Gaudi himself said: ‘The threads (of a hanging model) cannot

determine envelopes, since the former are lines and the latter are

surfaces, i.e. elements of a higher order’ (Codinachs, 1982: 106).



Candela observed correctly that Gaudi aimed, in this

particular design, for a structure loaded by pure compression.

An essential mistake, however, is the false interpretation of

the cloth as if it were introduced in the model in order to

interpret plate forces. As an historian, one may ask: What

source other than the description by Collins (1960) may have

led Candela to his misconception that Gaudi used cloth —

which Candela interpreted as plates — instead of linear

elements?

wrote: ‘gravitation and membrane forces subsequently achieve

the prospected equilibrated shape’ (Isler, 1992: 63). He also

stipulated that the model process requires a great deal of

manual care and experience if it is to yield shapes of high

precision.



From the point of view of statics, Isler’s form-finding process

produces hanging membrane shapes that are influenced by the

rubber stresses as well as by the forces of the floating and

hardening gypsum in a ‘magical’ interaction.

If one considers the hanging model form-finding methods

employed by Gaudi, Otto and Isler, one can detect a specific

difference from the goals of Candela. Candela was not in search

of the ideal shapes of vaulted structures involving only

compression forces. He applied shell theory and thus developed

his own personal calculation method for thin shells, a method in

which the ruled surface shape was helpful because at any point in

the structure he could look for intersections of two straight force

vectors. On the other hand, Otto and Isler applied hanging

models for form-finding in the case of a so-called ‘compression

shell’, in which the convenience of mere compression forces was

thought more appropriate than a shell structure that combined

shear and compression forces. Otto may be considered to have

followed the hanging model principle more consistently, whereas

Isler added other modelling effects so as to increase his shape

vocabulary. Gaudi himself, in contrast, followed the hanging

model visionary philosophy most carefully in his innovative

hanging model, maybe because he also lacked other options,

such as modern calculation methods, as a final control. What all

four engineering artists have in common is that they developed

their own personal design methods that they trusted as being safe

and were all reluctant to follow other methods simply because

they lacked personal experience of them.



3.3. The search for the proper surface form resulting

from the hanging model



The force lines respond to static requirements. Claiming that from

the threads arise the architectural shapes is infantile, since they only

represent a way of verifying stability, which one needs at a

convenient moment. However, before stability, other things have to

be satisfied: (space) capacity, lighting, sanitary requirements etc.

3.4 Gaudinian forms of geometric origin



Beside the catenary and polyhedra, in interviews Gaudi

referred to the following geometrical shapes: cone, cylinder,

ellipsoid, helicoid, hyperboloid, elliptic hyperboloid, parabo-

loid, hyperbolic paraboloid, sphere, cube, ellipse, hyperbola

and parabola as well as circle, line and point. A description of

the second-order surfaces — also known as quadratic surfaces

or quadrics — can be found in Hilbert and Cohn-Vossen (1999:

Chapter 1).



One group contains the two-dimensional conic sections

(Figure 13) and those three-dimensional bodies that are

derived by rotation from the conic sections: cone, cylinder,

ellipsoid, one- and two-sheet hyperboloid, paraboloid, cylinder

and sphere. A second category comprises three-dimensional



224



bodies derived from moving a straight line through space and

hence called ‘ruled surfaces’ (Figure 11): cone, cylinder, helicoid,

hyperboloid, elliptic hyperboloid (Figure 12), paraboloid and

hyperbolic paraboloid, with their intrinsic conic sections. Since

both groups are firmly related to the conic sections (Figure 8),

they may be considered as one single geometric category.



Actually, Gaudi made very little use of mathematics for these

shapes, which he visualised for the Sagrada Familia design by

high-precision gypsum models (Figures 8-10). As Gaudi stated

(Codinachs, 1982: 108):



The necessary geometry for executing surfaces does not complicate,

but rather simplifies the construction process; ‘complicate’ is the

algebraic expression of geometric things that are not completely

possible, which gives occasion to misunderstandings — which,

however, disappear when observing such bodies in space.



3.5 The concept of a skeleton covered by flesh for

structural parts



Gaudi considered the relationship between the engineering



shape and the architectural shape in a more fundamental way



(Puig Boada, 1981: 103):



Architecture does not equal stability: the latter is only a part of the

first, not coinciding totally. An iron bridge is statically correct but

not good (in an artistic sense). Architecture is art; statics is the

skeleton, the spine (of a structure), but it lacks the flesh — or the

envelope forms — which establish harmony and finally art.



Here, Gaudi lacks respect for the pure engineering form, which

later architects and engineers share.



The importance of these words is their differentiation between a

more or less abstract skeleton of (dimensionless) force lines and

the surrounding flesh (Figures 20 and 22), to which one might

add the image of a third element — the skin. In this respect,

the skin is the final surface structure, the colour and even

naturalistic sculpture — all important aspects of Gaudinian.

architecture.

structures in question are diaphragm walls with an arched

hole and thus, from a statical point of view, solvable with

different advantageous arch forms (Bassegoda Nonell,

1989; Roca et al. 1996).



A similar sense of differentiation is missed by those scholars.

who seek to fit Gaudinian shapes into architectural history.

As examples, one can cite references by Salvador Tarragé



(Tarragé, 1977; 13-59) and Carlos Flores (Flores, 1982:

223) to the tensile structures that were developed by Frei

Otto in collaboration with others and built in the roofs

of buildings for the Munich Olympic Games of 1972.

However, although the saddle-shape of a hyperbolic para-

boloid and the modern tent roof — in reality a pre-stressed

anti-clastic tensile structure — look much alike, they are,

both statically and by geometric description, quite foreign

to one another.



Such tensile structures are derived — at least theoretically — from

the geometrical shape of a minimal surface that may be

reproduced experimentally by a soap film. The minimal surface

as a soap skin experiment is — like the chain — one of the so-called

self-form-finding processes that have specific static relevance for

structural optimisation. In the case of the minimal surface, it is

the equal tension magnitude in the membrane on each point that

is translated into a textile membrane under regular tension or

into a net of pre-stressed steel cables (Gass, 1991). Ruled

surfaces lack similar intrinsic physical qualities and, as such,

they have no optimising effect when applied to a structural

shape, as with the shape of a simple cube for a house. These

structures require an additional statical calculation process

before building.

3.9 Graphical statical calculation applied to the

Sagrada Familia church



The force lines of the Sagrada Familia were found with graphical

methods, whereas those of the Colonia Giiell (church) were found

by experiment [the hanging model}; but Gaudi affirms that both

procedures are equal (Martinell, 1969: 39).



This crucial testimony (made in an interview with César

Martinell on 2 February 1915) is frequently falsely interpreted

as supporting the view that the Sagrada Familia was also

designed with a hanging model (Burry, 2007: figure 6, pp. 68-69).

But this is simply impossible: the Sagrada Familia has a neo-

gothic-based regular grid of equal vaults and tower positions that

are not in line with the synthetic character of the hanging model,

in which every weight part actively reflects the existence of others.

Another clue is that, in the Sagrada Familia, graphical statical

calculation tension forces occur that are not capable of being

represented in a hanging model (Sugrafies, 1923) (Figure 22).



Gaudi’s argument that the graphical statical calculation and the

hanging model are ‘equal’ may be interpreted as meaning that

both confirm the shape of the force lines and the force

magnitudes caused by gravity loading. On the other hand, there

is a major difference between the two methods: whereas the
